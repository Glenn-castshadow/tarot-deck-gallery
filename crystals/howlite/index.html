What Howlite means

Named for the Nova Scotian chemist Henry How, who first described it in 1868, howlite grows as knobby white nodules veined with grey like cracked porcelain. Because it takes dye so readily, much howlite has been coloured to pass as turquoise or lapis, calm wearing someone else's colours.

Emotional properties

A racing mind rarely responds well to being told to stop, and howlite does not try that approach either. It works more like a hand smoothing a rumpled blanket, one slow pass at a time, quieting an overactive temper before it boils into words you would take back later. Keep a piece in your pocket through a week stacked with small irritations, and let its pale, porous surface absorb some of the friction. By Friday the edge is duller than it was on Monday.

Spiritual properties

Your thoughts will wander within a minute of sitting down with howlite, and that is fine. Cup it in an open palm, and each time you notice you have drifted, trace one of its grey veins with a fingertip and begin again. The veins run everywhere and end nowhere, a fair map for a practice built on coming back. Twenty returns in one sitting means the practice is working.

Physical properties

Soft for a stone, howlite has a dry, chalky feel in a raw nodule, closer to unglazed clay than to a hard gem. Hold it for a minute before bed, as a pale full stop to the day. None of the glassy shock of harder stones lives in this dull, porous surface, only a plain, undemanding softness.

Care and cleansing

Much howlite is dyed, and a long soak can lift that colour or leave the stone patchy, so rinse it briefly rather than submerging it. A cloth-lined drawer spares its soft surface from scratches, and a night on a bed of clear quartz recharges it.
